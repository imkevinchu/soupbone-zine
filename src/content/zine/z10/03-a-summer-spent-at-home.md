In Dutch 17th century painting, still life straddled life and death. It often featured decay. The events of this summer and the trajectory of current events have left me feeling helpless and confined. I spent most of my time studying for an exam that is
considered alternatively corrupt, useless, and (at best) bad. In the interim, I cannot be the advocate that I was trained to be because I do not have a license. I cannot march openly because I cannot be arrested without losing a license I do not yet have.
This summer has been limbo. So, I have photographed fruit and my apartment. My pets and my partner.

Like Dutch 17th century painters, we are bearing witness to excess and decay. One of the current administration’s insidious goals has been to sow discord, condemn action, and convince us all that we are living at the end of the world. We are not, so
our action matters. It is selfish to think that we are living at the end of history. 

When we create, we leave little pieces of our most intimate selves in our handiwork. When we then share that work with others, we are being vulnerable and broadening conversation. Golden-age Dutch painting often incorporated symbols of industry and politics within more mundane imagery of the home, both in still life and in more comic domestic scenes. It reflected pride in the country’s status and criticism of the risks of greed and rapid growth.

Since I’ve lived in California, I’ve studied California agriculture and water. California’s agriculture feeds the world, often at the expense of people and the environment. Unlike other agricultural states, California farms primarily high-dollar investment crops, including avocados, stone fruit, grapes, and tree nuts. Citrus farming has become a private equity investment, not a passion for feeding people. Vineyards maximize sweetness for cotton candy table grapes and for 15% abv California cabernet and zinfandel. Sky-high revenue from stone fruit and nuts means that permanent orchards are replacing seasonal grain crops that allowed farmers to fallow land when water was scarce to replenish aquifers and enable seasonal fish migration in the north state. California agriculture is constantly exploding into economic bubbles reminiscent of Dutch tulip mania, but which continue to be propped up by the unwavering belief of big investors that technology can out-smart nature. Unfortunately, for all the eons that we have tried, we still cannot make it rain. The allure of raisins, oranges, almonds, and pistachios have transformed California’s agricultural land and rerouted its rivers to maximize profit. Still, we are running out of water.

In this project, I tried to capture both the frustration and loneliness of studying for the bar while knowing that this country’s woes live even inside an e-z peel orange. I tried to follow the Dutch masters—playing with shadow, the temporality of fresh fruit, and the comedy of domesticity. I also hoped to capture the beauty of a small life, the joy of ripe fruit and good wine because I cannot resist without surviving and I cannot survive without beauty.

By making art and sharing good food, we are making the world a more beautiful place and imagining a better future.

—Thalia Taylor, J.D.
